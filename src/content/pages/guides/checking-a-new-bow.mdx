## Why bother checking at all

A bow at this price is made to a price. That is not an accusation, it is what $100 to $200 buys, and none of the makers on the [beginner takedown comparison](/bows/takedown-recurve/) publishes a manufacturing tolerance for anyone to hold them to.

So the specification tells you what the bow is meant to be. These checks tell you what arrived.

Every one of them takes a few minutes, and the first is worth doing before you shoot a single arrow.

## Check the draw weight against the marked figure

**This is the one that matters.** Everything else on this page is worth knowing. This one changes whether you can learn to shoot.

Draw weight is marked at 28 inches. If you draw 26 inches you are pulling roughly 5 lb less than the label, and if you draw 30 you are pulling more. One seller on the comparison page states the rate directly: the Ghost is published as moving about 2.5 lb per inch either side of 28.

Hook a draw weight scale to the string, draw to your own draw length, and read it.

**Why it matters more than it sounds.** A beginner who orders 25 lb and receives 28 lb does not conclude the bow is mismarked. They conclude they are weak, or that their form is wrong, and they fight a bow that is too heavy while building the habits that come from fighting it. Too much draw weight early causes injuries and teaches faults, which is the whole argument of the [poundage guide](/guides/what-poundage-for-barebow/).

The bow does not have to be faulty for this to bite. A correctly made 25 lb limb pair is still 28 lb to someone with a 30 inch draw.

## Check the limbs are not twisted

Brace the bow, stand it upright, and look down the string from one limb tip to the other.

The string should run down the centre of both limbs and through the centre of the riser. If it sits off to one side on one limb, or the limb visibly rotates away from the string plane, the limb is twisted.

**A twisted limb is a return, not a tuning problem.** It will not shoot straight and you cannot fix it by adjusting anything. Photograph it against the string and contact the seller.

This check costs nothing and needs no tools, and it is the one a first-time buyer is least likely to know to do.

## Check the brace height

Brace height is the distance from the string to the deepest part of the grip. Measure it with a bow square.

Compare it against what the seller publishes. Most do not publish anything, which is a finding in itself: on the [comparison page](/bows/takedown-recurve/), only two sellers of ten state a brace height on the specification.

**A bow braced too low is loud and rough. Braced too high it loses speed and can stress the limbs.** Either way a beginner blames themselves for a bow that feels wrong, when the string is simply twisted to the wrong length.

Brace height is adjusted by twisting or untwisting the string, so this one is fixable at home. It is worth recording what it was when it arrived, because a Dacron string stretches over the first weeks and the figure moves.

## Weigh it

A kitchen scale reading grams is enough.

Mass weight decides whether you can hold the bow up at the end of a session, which is when form falls apart. It is also the specification almost nobody publishes, so weighing it is often the only way to know.

Weigh it unstrung, which is the convention makers use when they publish a figure at all.

## Check every bolt, then check them again

Limb bolts, and the thumb bolts or fittings on a no-tools takedown, work loose. Tighten them before the first session and check them again after it.

This is not a defect. A new bow settles.

## What tiller tells you, and what it does not

Tiller is the difference between the distance from the string to each limb butt, measured perpendicular to the string, with the bow braced.

Measure both, subtract, and you have it. On the one bow measured on this site, a PSE Razorback Jr., [tiller came out at +1/16 in](/bows/youth-recurve/), which is a small positive tiller and says the two limbs are reasonably well matched.

**This is a signal about the maker, not an action for you.** Nobody returns a bow over 1/16 of an inch, and a beginner cannot do anything useful with the number. It is on this page because it is the only number here that says anything about how carefully the bow was built, and because no maker at this price reports it.

If you shoot barebow and string walk, tiller becomes something you set deliberately rather than something you inspect. That is a later problem.

## What none of this tells you

These checks describe the bow in your hands on the day it arrived. They do not tell you whether the next one off the line is the same, and they are not a verdict on the maker.

One bow is one bow. This site says so on its own measurements too: a single measured figure is evidence about that item and nothing more.

What it does give you is the ability to tell a bad bow from your own bad form, in the first week, which is exactly when a beginner cannot tell the difference and usually assumes the worse of the two.
