## Buy the bow as one box, not as five purchases

A barebow setup is a riser, a pair of limbs, a string, an arrow rest, a tab and arrows. Written as a shopping list that reads like five decisions before you have shot anything.

It is not five decisions. **A complete takedown recurve is riser, limbs, string and rest in one box**, from $99.95, and you can shoot it the day it arrives. The [beginner takedown recurve comparison](/bows/takedown-recurve/) compares nine of them on published specifications, with the price and the date it was read next to each.

Buying the parts separately is a real route, and it is the one most barebow archers end up on. It is not the one to start on, and this site does not yet compare risers and limbs sold on their own.

## What a complete takedown recurve does not include

**A tab.** It protects the drawing fingers and, for barebow, carries the marks used to string walk. None of the nine complete bows compared on this site includes one.

**Arrows.** Matched to your draw length and to the draw weight of the bow, not bought generically. None of the nine complete bows includes arrows either.

This site does not yet publish checked prices for tabs or for arrows. When it does, the figures will carry the shop they came from and the day they were read, like every other price here. Until then, treat any number you find elsewhere as unchecked.

## What barebow bans, so you do not buy it by accident

Barebow bans a sight, a clicker, and stabilisers. It does not ban a plunger, and it does not ban weights added to the riser.

The [barebow rules guide](/guides/barebow-rules/) covers all of this with the rulebook article numbers, and is worth reading before you buy anything, because the difference between barebow, traditional and Olympic recurve equipment is not obvious from a retailer's category page. The [barebow vs traditional guide](/guides/barebow-vs-traditional/) and the [Olympic recurve vs barebow guide](/guides/olympic-recurve-vs-barebow/) both exist for exactly that confusion.

Most people buying a first bow are shooting in a garden and never enter a competition. None of these rules applies until you do. They matter at the moment of purchase anyway, because equipment bought without knowing them is equipment you replace.

## What can wait

**A plunger.** It is legal in barebow, and it is easy to assume otherwise if you have read about other no-sight divisions. It is tuning equipment rather than a requirement, and two of the nine complete bows compared on this site do not publish a plunger thread at all.

**Weights.** Useful once the bow is set up and you know what balance you are missing. Not needed to shoot your first arrows.

**A second set of limbs.** Buy one set, learn on it, and only add a second poundage once you know you are staying with the sport.

**Anything marketed as premium.** The expensive option is not the better choice for a first setup. It is the choice for an archer who already knows exactly what they need.

## If you are buying for a child

The question is not which bow shoots best. It is whether the bow grows.

[Youth recurve bows](/bows/youth-recurve/) run from $49.99 to $149.98, and the split is between takedown bows that accept heavier limbs on the same riser and one-piece bows fixed at one draw weight forever.
