## What arrow spine actually is

Spine is how much an arrow shaft bends under a given load. A lower spine number means a stiffer shaft. A higher spine number means a more flexible one. The number on the shaft is not a length or a weight; it is a stiffness rating specific to how that maker tests it.

A shaft that is too flexible for your setup will not clear the riser cleanly on release. A shaft that is too stiff will not flex enough to correct itself around the riser. Both show up as poor arrow flight, and both are usually blamed on the archer's form before anyone checks the arrows.

## The four things that change which spine you need

None of these can be looked at alone. A change to any one of them changes what spine the other three call for.

**Draw weight.** More draw weight bends the shaft more at release, so a heavier draw generally calls for a stiffer shaft, a lower spine number.

**Arrow length.** A longer arrow flexes more for the same draw weight, so length and spine move together. The same shaft cut two inches longer behaves as a weaker spine than the maker's chart lists for it.

**Point weight.** A heavier point loads the front of the shaft and increases the effective flex during the shot, so heavier points generally call for a stiffer shaft.

**Bow type.** A recurve riser and a barebow setup with added weights load a shaft differently than a compound does, which is why spine charts are split by bow type rather than shared across all of them.

Change your draw weight, your arrow length, your point weight, or your bow, and the spine you need changes with it.

## Why this site will not give you a spine number

This site does not run a spine calculator and will not compute a recommended spine from your draw weight and arrow length.

Two reasons, both practical. Makers do not use one shared spine standard. A 500 spine from one maker and a 500 spine from another are tested differently and are not guaranteed to behave the same. A generic formula would be presenting someone else's estimate as this site's own measurement, which is exactly what CLAUDE.md rule 4 exists to prevent.

The honest version of this page points you at real published charts and real people, not a calculator.

## Read the maker's own chart when one is published

Some makers publish a complete spine chart. The [Victory VAP Target Sport V6](/arrows/), covered on the arrows comparison page, lists ten spine sizes from 400 to 1200 with the grains per inch for each one, straight from the maker's own listing on [Lancaster Archery Supply](#source-lancaster-shafts).

Where a chart like that exists, it is the right source to use, because it reflects how that specific maker rates that specific shaft. A chart from a different maker's shaft is not a substitute.

## When no chart is published, ask before you buy

Not every maker publishes one. The arrows comparison page found two shafts, both from Easton, that publish a spine size range with no per-spine weight chart on the retail listing. The most expensive shaft on that page, the Easton X10, is normally spine-matched by a coach or pro shop rather than chosen from a public chart at all.

If you cannot find a published chart for a shaft you are considering, that is a sign to ask a pro shop or a coach before ordering a dozen, not a gap to fill with a guess.

## What to do next

Weigh your setup, draw weight, arrow length and point weight, against the actual chart for the shaft you are buying, not a number carried over from a different maker or a different length.

If you shoot barebow, remember that your setup includes any weights added to the riser, which changes how the whole bow loads the shaft compared with the same riser bare.
